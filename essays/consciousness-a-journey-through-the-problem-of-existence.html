Consciousness — A Journey Through the Problem of Existence

13 August 2026 · 1 min read

Consciousness is an idea which has eluded the understanding of many a great thinker of the past, from Descartes to Chalmers. Although throughout the years many attempts have been made to explain this phenomenon, both philosophical and psychological, it remains yet a black box. It may be considered ironic: that something humans experience every moment, the phenomenon that makes a human, is so poorly understood to date despite the best efforts of humanity. We must exercise caution in asking the naïve question, “What is consciousness?”; it may never be answered satisfactorily in our lifetime, perhaps never; still, the journey we take, the arguments we put forward as an attempt to answer this very naïve question, open many facets and outlets to discuss the problem of existence in its entirety. This essay attempts to: foremost, ask appropriate questions, build upon the works of various philosophers, and arrive at a conclusion that tries to answer the problem of existence.

It is appropriate now to establish a link between the consciousness problem and the problem of existence — how, by shifting our focus to the problem of existence, we can get a clearer picture of consciousness. The idea of consciousness and existence are fundamentally interlinked. One cannot imagine an existence without consciousness, as an existence without a conscious observer to observe it is meaningless.

Descartes' Mind-Body Dualism

Descartes, primarily a mathematician and anatomist, strived to know the truth above all; and being a man of science, the only path to a universal truth was a path of logic and reason. Descartes posits that reasoning is the primary source of true knowledge. In his pursuit of logical truth, Descartes attempted to introduce an axiomatic approach to explain existence. His radical doubt was the basis of his philosophy. He extended his doubt to such an extent that even his own existence came into jeopardy — it is in this context Descartes gave perhaps the most well-known quote in the entirety of the western philosophical system: “Cogito, ergo sum” (I think, therefore I am).

Through this quote, Descartes establishes the existence of the mind. Something known as the “mind” must exist, as it is that very thing which does the thinking. The only thing Descartes is certain of is the existence of the mind. According to him, the existence of the outside world or “body” is dubious. As the mind and body possess different attributes, they must be separate. Therefore, it leads to a dualist approach.

Sartre's Radical Freedom

Sartre was a staunch proponent of the idea that humans, as conscious beings, are free to make choices which define their existence. He said there is no pre-determined purpose or nature for humans, and that our essence is shaped by our actions, choices, and freedom. This idea is beautifully distilled into three words: “Existence precedes essence.”

Conversely, it is arguable that free will is immaterial without the existence of consciousness, as there must be a force that propels the action of decision-making. From these arguments, it may be inferred that consciousness gives rise to existence. The reader may have noticed that Sartre has been contradicted here. It has been concluded that without consciousness, existence cannot be — without the existence of consciousness, the freedom Sartre posits would not have come into being.

This creates a problem. Neither of the two arguments presented here is wrong; they are both correct in their place, but it leaves one feeling a sense of dissatisfaction. This suggests that instead of viewing consciousness and existence as separate entities, they might be better understood as two inseparable aspects of the same phenomenon.

A Solution to Resolving Sartre's Paradox

As we saw, Sartre is not wrong in his arguments — rather it is the error of assuming consciousness and existence as separate which is causing the paradox. A simple solution is to look at consciousness and existence as two parts of a whole rather than trying to differentiate between the two. In his philosophy, Heidegger does precisely that. Rather than looking at consciousness and existence as separate, he combines the two in his idea of Dasein.

Heidegger's Rejection of Descartes

Descartes, in his work Meditations on First Philosophy, establishes the dual nature of body and mind — existence and consciousness. He claimed that the mind is separate from the physical world, and that they are fundamentally separate. Heidegger rejected this mind-body dualism in his work Being and Time by stating that consciousness, rather than being a separate entity from the universe, is in the universe; they exist as Dasein, meaning actually being there — consciousness is embedded inside existence.

We consciously interact with the universe around us by being there. Hence rather than saying “I think, therefore I am,” Heidegger suggests “I am; therefore I think.” According to him, existence is in consciousness. In his arguments, Heidegger destroys the Cartesian idea that thinking or consciousness is the foundation of being — rather it's a mode of being that arises from experience. In his critique of Descartes' view of humanity, Heidegger does not directly challenge the cogito itself. Instead, Heidegger considers humans as possessing an inherent understanding of Being. His argument is that using the ergo cogito as the foundation of philosophy leaves the sum (the “I am”) undefined and lacking clarity.

Husserl's Phenomenology

In the previous section we saw how Heidegger rejects Descartes and his gravitation towards a monistic existence. He was greatly influenced by Husserl's idea of “Phenomenology,” and he in fact was himself a student of Husserl. Husserl, in his ideas of phenomenology, diverted quite a bit from the ideas of his peers and contemporaries and returned to Plato. Phenomenology may be understood as the objective study of the subjective — in this case, consciousness.

Husserl sought to formalize this phenomenological method by incorporating the idea of Epoché, or bracketing. Epoché is that method by which we suspend all assumptions about the external world and focus purely on how we, as conscious beings, experience “phenomenon.” Husserl posits that consciousness cannot exist in a vacuum; to give existence to consciousness there must be things the consciousness is experiencing — the outside world, or “phenomenon.” On the basis of these arguments, it may be inferred that it is only through the intentionality of consciousness that we exist and give the outside world meaning.

The reader may have noticed striking similarities and parallels between Husserl's phenomenology and Plato's Allegory of the Cave. In his allegory of the cave, Plato posits that there exists a world of ideas, and we as humans only get an impression of that idea through our consciousness — it's only our consciousness which gives existence to the outside world. Whereas Husserl says that we can experience the world only through our consciousness.

Hegel's Absolute Spirit

Hegel was a German philosopher who was influenced deeply by the idealistic philosophy of Kant and Descartes, which states that only ideas can be sure to exist. He was also a rationalist who gave the quote “What is rational, is real.” Hegel expanded upon the idealist ideas of Descartes and Kant and paved the path towards Absolute Idealism, another solution to Sartre's paradox explored earlier.

In Absolute Idealism, consciousness and existence are not separate entities — rather they compose one single thing which Hegel calls Geist, or the Absolute Spirit. It is from this Geist that consciousness emerges. Hegel employs the dialectic method as an explanation of how consciousness materializes from the Geist. The dialectic method may be understood, in its most basic sense, like a dialogue between two parties: the first party puts forward a “thesis,” and the second party puts forth the “antithesis.” Through the process of contradiction, negation, and modification, one arrives at a “synthesis,” or truth.

Hence Hegel's Absolute Idealism attempts to explain an all-encompassing entity called Geist, of which consciousness and existence are reflections. Hegel's Absolute Idealism may also be thought of as the bridge between western and Indian philosophy. The philosophy of Advaita Vedanta explored similar ideas of atman and paramatman to Hegel's consciousness and Geist.

From Geist to Brahman: Hegel and Advaita Vedanta

As we saw in the previous section, Hegel conceptualized the idea of the Geist, which has a similar monistic essence to the Indian philosophical idea of Advaita Vedanta. In this section we'll see similarities between the two, with a tilt towards Advaita Vedanta — the philosophy of the influential Indian philosopher Adi Shankara. Advaita literally means “non-secondness,” but in practice the meaning is understood more as “non-dualism.”

Where the western world struggled with the duality of mind and body, existence and consciousness, Advaita Vedanta claimed that the Brahman is the absolute truth, and consciousness, existence, essence, mind, and body are nothing but the illusions and reflections of this ultimateness. Adi Shankara called the world maya, or an illusion. The atman, or the self, is posited as the “pure consciousness”; this atman emerges from the paramatman, which is considered the essence of the universe. Upon the death of an individual, the atman returns to the paramatman, and the conscious existence of the individual ceases to be.

These bridging concepts of Geist and paramatman should perhaps be understood as one and the same — and it is probably in them where we shall find the answer to our problem of existence.

Conclusion

Throughout this journey, we looked at and explored the philosophical ideas of various great thinkers, diverting from them momentarily in the pursuit of true knowledge. We talked about Descartes' mind-body dualism, Sartre's existentialism, and his idea of radical freedom. We deduced how talking about consciousness and existence as separate, and keeping a dualist mindset, led to Sartre's Paradox. In an attempt to solve Sartre's Paradox, we saw how it will be more appropriate to look at consciousness and existence as the same, and to keep a monist mindset.

We further expanded on the monistic idea by employing the help of Husserl's phenomenology, which led us naturally to Hegel's Absolute Idealism. It was also interesting to point out the bridge between western and Indian philosophy — the similarities between Geist and paramatman, and the hypothesis that perhaps Geist and paramatman are two words for the same absolute truth.

If consciousness and existence are but reflections of the same ultimate reality, then what may be its nature? Could human consciousness ever even truly understand its own nature? Maybe not — for the ant understands not the workings of the cosmos.
